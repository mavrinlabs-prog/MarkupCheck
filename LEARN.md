## The idea in one paragraph
Dealers can add a markup to the interest rate a lender approved, and buyers can't see the approved rate. Lenders that sell car loans into public bonds must file every loan's details with the SEC. We collected 1,978,371 of those loans from 29 deals by 10 lenders, kept 554,021, and trained a model to answer: for a buyer like you, what APR did similar real loans get, and is your offer above that? We also asked whether public data alone can catch a markup.

## Each step: what it does, why it's there, and what would go wrong without it

1. **SEC filings (Regulation AB II, Form ABS-EE, exhibit EX-102).** Real loan-level records (score, APR, car, term) that lenders must publish. Without them we'd have only averages or made-up data.
2. **Each deal's *first* pool filing.** Monthly updates drop loans that were paid off or defaulted, and the risky, high-APR loans disappear first. A later file would make prices look lower than they were.
3. **Only loans made within 12 months before the filing.** Pools also hold years-old loans that survived payoffs and defaults, so they aren't typical.
4. **Cleaning with every dropped row counted.** We removed promo (subsidised) rates, business borrowers, records without a consumer score, and impossible values. The notebook prints how many rows each rule removed.
5. **Target = APR minus the 5-year Treasury rate that month (the "spread").** Interest rates across the economy moved between 2023 and 2026. This stops the model from mistaking "loans were cheaper in 2023" for "this buyer got a better deal".
6. **Inputs: credit score, new/used, car age, amount, loan-to-value, term.** These are all things the buyer knows. **Payment-to-income is left out**, because the payment is calculated from the APR, so it would leak the answer. **State and lender are left out** of the main model so we can audit them.
7. **Split by time, never at random.** We train on Jan 2023–Nov 2024, calibrate on Dec 2024–Jul 2025, and test on Aug 2025–Jan 2026 (112,657 loans, 6 months). A random split would let the model learn from the future. Toyota, Nissan and Capital One have no test-month loans (their newest deals' loans were all made before August 2025), so the test covers the other seven lenders.
8. **Baselines.** These are the simple rules the model has to beat:
   - B0: one range for everyone.
   - B1: the range for your credit tier and new/used, which is what a rate table gives you.
9. **Quantile regression.** A normal model predicts the average. A quantile model predicts a chosen point: "10% of similar loans were below this", "half were", "90% were". We train 19 of them (5%, 10%, … 95%), using gradient-boosted trees: many small decision trees, each fixing the last one's mistakes. The settings (learning rate, tree size) are fixed in `src/mc.py` and were never tuned. Each loan's 19 predictions are sorted before use, so a lower quantile can never come out above a higher one (`scoring.predict_grid`).
10. **Conformal calibration.** On calibration loans the model didn't train on, we widen the 10%–90% range by the smallest amount (`qhat`, 0.35 points) that makes it contain 80% of them. On the later test months it contained 81.7% overall, measured, not assumed. It held for less in some groups (76.3% for prime used-car buyers), and the app shows each group's own number.
11. **The two flags.** "High" means the APR is above the range. "Well above" means more than `delta` (1.08 points) above it. `delta` is the smallest margin that flags no more than 2.5% of ordinary calibration loans, a false-alarm budget set before looking at test loans.
12. **Markup-injection test (our own question).** We can't see real markups, so we add +1, +2 or +2.5 points to a random 10% of test loans and measure:
    - precision: how many flags are right;
    - recall_new: how many markups are newly caught, not counting loans that were already above the range before the markup.

    At +2 points, "well above" newly caught 10.8% with 5.5% false alarms (10.6–11.5% across 50 random draws). At that same false-alarm rate the B1 rule caught 4.9% (with its own threshold it caught 7.8% at 8.5% false alarms). The looser "high" flag caught 20.1% with 10.1% false alarms.

    Two assumptions: the markup sizes and the 10% share are ours (if only 2% of loans were marked up, precision would fall from 24% to 5%), and real loans may already carry markups, so "typical" includes them and some "false alarms" may be real markups. **Answer: mostly no, with these six inputs.** The range similar loans span averages 8.1 points, about four times a 2-point markup.
13. **Repayment test.** It covers only the original three lenders (Ally, CarMax, Santander), not the seven added later. We followed their 2024 deals' loans for 12 months, matching on deal *and* asset number (asset numbers repeat across deals). Those loans were in the training months, so each loan's comparison line came from a model that never saw that loan (5-fold cross-fitting). High-priced loans later went 30+ days late or were charged off more often: 23.9% vs 14.2% overall; Santander 42.1% vs 29.7%; Ally 6.3% vs 3.1%; CarMax 8.6% vs 7.2%. So a high price can also mean risk the data can't see. It could also mean a markup raised the payment, and this test can't separate the two. The pooled gap (9.8 points) mixes lenders, because Santander has both more high-priced loans and more bad loans; within a lender the gap averages 5.7 points. A month with no status reported counts as "current" (5–14% of monthly rows, by lender); dropping every loan with a missing month still gives 31.3% vs 17.6%.
14. **Fairness on groups the model never sees.** The residual is the real spread minus the model's typical spread. For each lender and state we report its average residual minus the overall average, so positive means "paid more than similar borrowers on average", with bootstrap confidence intervals (500 resamples). With 43 states, about 2 would look significant by chance alone; 27 still do (within the same lender) after a Holm correction for testing 43 at once. The intervals resample single loans, not dealers or months, so they are somewhat too narrow.
15. **Model B, the measured improvement.** A markup sits on top of a *specific* lender's rate, so when the buyer names the lender we compare with that lender's own loans. We first compared the two models on test months while building, which is peeking. So we re-checked on calibration months, before the test period: B still won (error 1.50 vs 1.67; newly caught markups 32.7% vs 24.5%). On test: error 2.35 vs 2.53.
16. **Percentile.** "Higher than N% of similar loans" is read off the 19 quantiles. It's close to honest at the top (88.0% of test loans at or below the 90th percentile) but not at the bottom (14.7% below the 10th). A correction we tried made the test months worse, so the app doesn't use it.
17. **The app's "reasons".** For each input, we change it to a typical value and see how much the predicted typical rate moves. It's a comparison, not proof of cause.
18. **How sure are these numbers (notebook section 7c).**
    - The model beat the rule in all 6 test months and at 6 of the 7 lenders with test loans (not Hyundai, 210 loans); its error is 0.97 points lower (95% interval 0.95–0.98). With the lender named (the same-lender model) it beat the rule at all 7. At Hyundai the all-lenders model's loss is real, 0.35 points worse than the rule (95% interval 0.21–0.49, `src/later_deals.py`), and naming the lender fixes it (0.13 better, 0.02–0.24), so the page tells buyers who leave the lender blank.
    - A straight-line model on the same six inputs scored 3.24, against 2.56 for trees on the same 30,000 loans, so the trees matter, not only the extra inputs.
    - Error grows from 1.73 (Aug 2025) to 3.78 (Jan 2026) because Santander, the hardest lender to price, goes from 6% to nearly all test loans.
    - Do car-loan rates follow the Treasury rate point for point? Our rough check gave 0.11 points per Treasury point (95% interval −0.50 to 0.68), and it is confounded by other changes over time, so we can't confirm it either way; the app says it assumes it.
19. **The app checks its own verdict ("How sure is this?").** Each check re-runs the same trained models with one input or assumption changed, and says whether the verdict stays the same. Nothing is retrained.
    - **Every lender.** The same offer next to each of the 10 lenders' own similar loans (model B), with the buyer's lender marked. A rate that is typical at one lender can be high at another: Sam's is typical at Santander and well above at the other nine.
    - **Credit score 30 points lower and higher.** Credit apps often show a VantageScore, while lenders use a FICO score; the two can differ. 30 points is our what-if size, not a measured gap.
    - **Rates unchanged since January 2026.** The app normally adds today's Treasury rate (4.93%, Oct 2026). This check uses January 2026's 3.78% instead, as if car-loan rates hadn't moved. Our own estimate (step 18) can't tell which is right, so we show both.
    - **New cars only: the car valued 5% lower** (some lenders record the invoice price, not the sticker), and, for a 'new' car more than a year old, **the same loan as a used car**.
    - **Evidence.** How many of the 554,021 loans match on new/used, score within about 20 points and term within about 6 months. Under 500 is flagged as few. The counts come from `src/build_extras.py`, which checks it used the same 554,021 loans.
    - **Risk.** The repayment test's late-or-charged-off rates for loans priced above 90% of similar loans (above the out-of-fold 90th percentile) against all other loans (step 13), for the three lenders it covers.
    - **Credit unions and banks.** NCUA's national average car-loan rates for December 26, 2025, for the closest term. They are not adjusted for credit score, so the app presents them as a place to get a quote, not as the buyer's rate. Newer, below them: each credit union's own posted rate from NCUA's Call Report for June 30, 2026 (median 5.44% new, 6.28% used; one posted rate per credit union, not tied to a score or term), and, for new cars, the Federal Reserve's G.19 bank rates (7.54% at 60 months and 7.17% at 72, 2026 Q3, preliminary). `src/fetch_ncua_callreport.py` and `src/fetch_g19.py` rebuild them.
    - **On newer loans.** The result says how the same model did on newer loans it never saw (step 21), so the buyer knows the range may sit too high for 2026 loans.
    - What it still can't fix: the data has no race, sex, age or dealer, and credit unions appear only as averages.
20. **The money tools (no new model, only loan arithmetic in `app/finance.py` and the trained models).**
    - **APR from a payment.** Dealers often quote a payment. The fixed-rate payment formula only goes up as the APR goes up, so we search for the APR that gives that payment (bisection, to well under 0.0001%). If you give both, we compare the payment with what the APR should cost; more than $1 a month higher means something else is in the payment.
    - **Add-ons** are financed at the loan's APR, so their real cost is their price plus the interest on them. **One point of APR** is the difference in total paid at APR and APR + 1.
    - **Ways to a lower rate and the sliders.** With each check, the model also scores 325 variations (13 credit scores × 5 terms × 5 down payments) in one pass, and the page's sliders read that grid, so moving them needs no server call. It's still "loans like this got", not a promise from a lender. A cell backed by fewer than 500 similar loans says "treat it with care" and is never offered as a way to a lower rate (for Jordan's 660 score on a used car, 84 months rests on only 256 loans).
    - **Charts.** The distribution is a smoothed histogram from the model's 19 quantiles. The "why" waterfall starts at a typical loan in the data and changes one input at a time; whatever the one-at-a-time bars don't explain is shown as "other and combined effects", so the bars add up.
    - **Dealer card and share link.** The link stores the numbers after the `#`, which browsers never send to a server, so the server never logs them. Anyone you send it to can read them. The printed card also carries the verdict, the cost gap, how sure it is, the data dates, a line saying it can't show a markup, and the full link as text.
    - **Rule of thumb beside the result.** The same credit tier × new/used rule as baseline B1 (step 8), rebuilt by `src/build_rule_of_thumb.py`, which stops unless the rule's test error matches the notebook's 3.499. For Sam the rule says 7.73% to 16.65%, so Sam's 14.5% would look normal on a rate table; the model's range is 6.93% to 13.03%.
    - **If the dealer pushes back, and written offers.** Fixed replies (no AI) filled with the buyer's own numbers, and a message the buyer sends themselves to two or three lenders so every quote is for the same loan. A test bans words like "overcharged" and any promised saving.
    - **The whole deal.** Price, anything financed beyond it, interest and the total of payments, from the buyer's own numbers; the tool doesn't judge the car's price.
    - **Contract-box check.** Federal rules (Regulation Z, 12 CFR 1026.17) group the APR, Finance Charge, Amount Financed and Total of Payments together on the contract. The check confirms that the Amount Financed plus the Finance Charge equals the Total of Payments, and that the APR matches the payments within 1/8 of a point, the accuracy rule in 1026.22(a)(2). A first payment due later than a month can explain a small difference, and the page says so.
    - **Your state.** Picking a state shows its same-lender gap (step 14) and its credit unions' median posted rate, computed in the browser from numbers sent with the result, with no server call.
    - **Refinance extras.** An optional rate a lender actually offered (the difference is shown "if approved at it"), and a calendar file with reminders 6 and 12 months out, built in the page, with no numbers from the check in it.
    - **Compare, Refinance, Batch.** Compare scores each offer against the whole market and ranks by total interest. Refinance treats the rest of a loan as a used-car loan for the months left; refinance loans can be priced differently, so the tab says so. Batch checks up to 500 rows from a CSV and returns a CSV.
    - **Explore.** The state map shows each state's same-lender gap; solid outlines are the 27 of 43 that survive the Holm correction (step 14). The tracker is raw monthly medians, so a jump can mean a different mix of loans that month.
    - **API.** The same check as JSON at `/check` (30 checks a minute per IP address, like the page). Responses carry `api_version`, the data months, the rule of thumb and the newer rates; the page's own handlers are left out of the API docs, so only `/check` is listed.
    - **Classroom mode ("For teachers").** Four made-up buyers (Jordan, Sam, Alex, Riley), a printable worksheet and an answer key that the model computes when the app starts, so the key always matches the page. Students type nothing personal.
    - **Partner demo (`?partner=example`).** A credit union could add its name, its posted rate and a link below the credit-union section. The page's own script adds it after the server's result arrives, so the result and every number in it are the same with or without a partner (a browser test compares them). The demo partner is a made-up name and shows NCUA's real median instead of a made-up rate. The footer says plainly that nobody pays MarkupCheck.
    - **Spanish.** The English/Español switch relabels every field and re-runs the result through `app/i18n.py`, a table of whole sentences with the numbers kept exactly. The API and the batch CSV stay in English.
    - **Security.** Every response carries security headers (a content policy that blocks other sites' scripts, no sniffing, no camera or microphone access), fonts are served by the app itself, Gradio's usage tracking is off, and a check is limited to 30 a minute per IP address (`app/web_security.py`). Gradio's run history is also off (`run_history=False` in `app/app.py`): left on, it keeps every check in the browser and lists it at `/gradio_api/runs`, so the next person on a shared computer could open the last buyer's offer.

21. **Tests after the build (`src/forward_test.py`, notebook section 7d).**
    - **Newer loans.** The shipped model, unchanged, on 113,200 CarMax loans originated Feb–Aug 2026, from three deals filed after training: typical-price error 1.72 against 2.45 for the rule of thumb (better than the rule in all 7 months), but the 80% range held for only 68.8% (65.7% for prime used). 23.9% were cheaper than the range and 7.3% above it, so newer loans were priced lower than the model expects. The fair comparison is CarMax's own loans in the original test months: 1.55, with 83.1% coverage. Keeping the 80% promise needs the range recalibrated on new filings (the last two bullets).
    - **A later deal from a lender in the data.** Capital One's 2024-1 and 2025-1 deals are now in the training data, so Prime 2026-1 (44,690 loans) tests a known lender's newer pool, not a lender the model never saw: error 1.19 against 1.45 for the rule, coverage 77.1%; its loans were 0.83 points cheaper than typical on average, so 22.3% fell below the range. With the lender named, the same-lender model did better: 0.95 against 1.45, coverage 84.1%.
    - **Later deals for the lenders with no test-month loans (`src/later_deals.py`).** Toyota's, Nissan's and Capital One's training deals end before the test months, so we scored deals they filed later, which the model never saw, the way the app does when a buyer names the lender. Toyota 2026-C (pool filed July 2026; 6,411 loans after cleaning, July 2025 to January 2026): error 1.07 against 1.38 for the rule, coverage 76.4%. Capital One 2026-1: 0.95 against 1.45, coverage 84.1%. Nissan 2026-A and 2026-B (51,130 and 54,150 loans) mark every loan as manufacturer-supported (a rate subsidy or a cash rebate), which cleaning removes for every lender, so Nissan can't be tested; the page says so. Each deal's row count matches its prospectus.
    - **Realistic markup sizes.** A 2016 federal order capped Toyota's dealers at 1.25 points (terms of 60 months or less) and 1.00 point (longer). At those caps, the "well above" flag newly caught 4.8% of marked-up loans, against 10.8% at +2.0; the rule of thumb, at the same false-alarm rate, caught 1.8%. None of these tests retrains a model.
    - **Calibrating each group separately (`src/eval_group_calibration.py`).** We set a separate width for each credit tier × new/used group on the first half of the calibration months, with the rule fixed beforehand: keep it only if, on the other half, the worst group gets closer to 80% and overall coverage stays between 78% and 82%. It didn't: the worst gap stayed at 18.5 points (deep-subprime new cars, with only 28 calibration loans, too few to get their own width), so again the app keeps one width. The test months were reported but not used to decide; there, prime used-car buyers would have stayed at 76.2%. The lesson: the prime-used shortfall comes from prices drifting over time, not from the width.
    - **Recalibrating on new filings (`src/eval_recalibration.py`).** On the newer CarMax loans, re-widening the whole-market range once on Feb–May 2026 lifted Jun–Aug coverage from 65.7% to 75.3%, short of the 78% we required beforehand, so the app doesn't use it. A monthly routine did better: recalibrating each month on the two months before it brought whole-market coverage from 65.4% to 76.8% (Apr–Aug, 79,173 loans) and cut the error from 1.77 to 1.61 points; for the same-lender view, coverage went from 59.2% to 77.2% and the error from 1.66 to 1.55.
    - **The app doesn't recalibrate CarMax (`src/recalibrate.py`).** Its rule: a lender's monthly recalibration goes into the app only if its pooled coverage is between 78% and 82% (the band `src/eval_recalibration.py` fixed before any recalibration was run), its error is no worse than the frozen model's, and its two newest months hold at least 2,000 loans. CarMax reached 77.2%, so it isn't applied, and the page shows the newer-loans warning instead. An earlier version used a 75–85% band, which CarMax passed, but we chose that band after seeing the first test, so we went back to the one set in advance. Capital One's and Toyota's later deals have no loans newer than January 2026, Nissan's are all promotional, and we haven't fetched newer filings for the other lenders.

## 31 questions judges will probably ask
1. **How do you know it's a markup and not risk?** We don't, for a single loan. Our repayment test (the original three lenders) shows high-priced loans were riskier, especially at Santander and Ally, so the app says a high APR can be markup or hidden risk and tells the buyer to ask for the buy rate and get a second offer.
2. **Why not use accuracy?** We predict a range, so we report error in points and coverage. For the rare-event markup test we report precision and recall, as the briefs ask, against a baseline rule.
3. **What about credit unions?** They usually don't sell loans into bonds, so they aren't in the loan data. The app shows NCUA's national credit-union and bank averages next to the offer, labelled as not adjusted for credit score, plus each credit union's posted rate from the June 2026 Call Report, nationally and by state.
4. **What can't your data tell you about fairness?** The filings have no race, sex or age. We audited lender and state, which the main model never sees, and say plainly that protected groups couldn't be checked.
5. **Why drop payment-to-income?** The payment comes from the APR, so it would leak the answer.
6. **Why split by time?** So we test on later loans the model never saw, like a real buyer next month.
7. **What is conformal calibration?** It widens the predicted range by the smallest amount that makes it contain 80% of held-out loans. We then measured it on later months: 81.7% overall, lower for some groups.
8. **Your model beats the rule by about a point. Is that useful?** It cuts the typical-price error by 28% with a narrower range that still covers 80%, and it wins in every test month and at 6 of the 7 lenders with test loans (not Hyundai, 210 loans); with the lender named it wins at all 7, and on later Toyota and Capital One deals. A straight-line model on the same inputs does worse (3.24 vs 2.56), so the gain isn't only from having more inputs. But it can't reliably catch a markup on its own.
9. **So does the tool work?** It tells a buyer where their offer sits among real loans and what the gap costs, and it beats the rule of thumb at that. Our own question's answer is that public data alone mostly can't prove a markup. That's a real finding: knowing what similar buyers paid isn't enough, on its own, to prove a markup.
10. **Did you tune on the test set?** We first compared the two models on test months while building. We disclosed that, and re-checked the choice on calibration months, where the lender model still won. The flag threshold and the range width were set on calibration loans only.
11. **Why these ten lenders?** They file large, regular deals with full loan-level data. The first three cover different markets: CarMax used cars, Ally a new/used mix, Santander subprime. We then added six carmakers' finance arms (Toyota, Honda, Hyundai, Nissan, Ford Credit, GM Financial) and a bank, Capital One, which file the same format.
12. **Your rates are from 2023–2026, but rates are higher now?** Yes. The app adds today's Treasury rate and says it assumes car-loan rates moved with it point for point. We tried to check that in our own months and got 0.11 points per Treasury point with a 95% interval of −0.50 to 0.68, a rough check confounded by other changes over time that can't settle it either way. So the app shows the verdict both ways: with rates following the Treasury, and with rates unchanged since January 2026. It also never shows an APR outside what the data contained.
13. **Why gradient-boosted trees?** They handle non-linear effects (a score drop matters more at 620 than at 800) and train in minutes on a laptop.
14. **What did you build yourselves vs use from tools?** We used scikit-learn for the model and Gradio for the app. Claude (Anthropic) helped research and compare ideas, check datasets, design the method, write, test and review code, translate the app into Spanish, and draft the write-up and video script. The question, the data choice, the tests and every decision in this file are ours to explain (see SOURCES.md).
15. **What next?** Recalibrate every lender monthly on new filings (CarMax came close: 77.2% against a 78% bar), test with real buyers' offers (with permission), and add non-bond lenders.
16. **Why is the error bigger in later test months?** Santander's share of test loans grows from 6% to nearly 100%, and Santander is the hardest lender to price. Per lender, the error is 1.45 (Ally), 1.47 (Honda), 1.55 (CarMax), 1.64 (Hyundai), 1.65 (GM Financial), 1.94 (Ford Credit) and 3.72 (Santander).
17. **You tested 43 states. Aren't some gaps luck?** About 2 would look significant by chance. 27 hold up after a Holm correction; Alabama and Connecticut both do. The intervals treat loans as independent, which they aren't quite (same dealer, same month), so they're a bit too narrow.
18. **Couldn't the "ordinary" loans already contain markups?** Yes. "Typical" means typical of real loans, markups included, and some "false alarms" may be real markups. CarMax is both the dealer and the lender on its own loans, so the classic markup may work differently there.
19. **How do you know one verdict isn't fragile?** The app re-checks it with a credit score 30 points either way, with rates unchanged since the data, at each lender, and for new cars with a lower car value. It says how many of those checks agree and how many similar loans back the result (step 19). The lender table is shown separately and isn't part of that count: a rate can be typical at one lender and high at another.
20. **How do the sliders answer instantly?** When you check an offer, the model also scores 325 variations of it at once and sends that grid with the page; the sliders just look up the nearest cell (step 20).
21. **A dealer quoted me a payment, not an APR. Can you still check it?** Yes. The app works out the APR that payment implies on your amount and term. If you have both, it flags a payment that is higher than the APR explains, which usually means add-ons or fees are inside it.
22. **When Sam sees "well above", what's the chance it really is a markup?** For one loan we can't know. In our test, if 10% of loans carried a 2-point markup, 24% of "well above" flags would be markups; if only 2% did, 5% would. That's why the label says "well above similar loans", not "marked up" (step 12).
23. **Isn't Sam cherry-picked right at the threshold?** Yes, on purpose: Sam's 14.5% is the first APR in 0.5-point steps that the model calls "well above" for that profile, and the app itself says the verdict changes in 1 of 3 checks. At 16.5% it holds in all three.
24. **Bond-pool loans are chosen by the lender. Are they representative?** No. They are loans ten lenders chose to sell into public bonds, which is why we name the lenders, show each group's measured coverage, and list non-bond lenders as the next step.
25. **Prime used buyers get 76% coverage, not 80%. Why not calibrate each group separately?** We tried it again with the new lenders, with the rule fixed beforehand. On held-back calibration months the worst group didn't get any closer to 80%, so the rule said no, and on the test months prime used buyers would have stayed at 76.2%. So the shortfall comes from prices drifting over time, not from the width. The fix that worked was recalibrating every month on new filings, which the app now does for CarMax (step 21). Elsewhere the app shows each group's measured coverage.
26. **Why a 2-point markup and a 10% share?** They are our assumptions, so we also ran +1 and +2.5 points: even at +2.5 points, "well above" newly caught only 16.3% (step 12).

27. **Does it still work on newer loans?** The typical price does: on 113,200 newer CarMax loans the error was 1.72 against 2.45 for the rule of thumb. The range doesn't: it held for 68.8%, because newer loans were cheaper, so an offer can look more typical than it is. The app says this under "How sure is this?". Recalibrating CarMax's same-lender range each month on the two months before brought it from 59.2% to 77.2% (step 21), just short of the 78–82% bar we set before running it, so the app doesn't apply it and warns instead. The other lenders need newer filings first.
28. **Isn't 2 points too big a markup?** Maybe. The only official anchor we found is a 2016 federal order capping Toyota's dealers at 1.25 or 1.00 points; at those caps the flag newly caught 4.8%. Smaller markups are even harder to see, which supports our answer: public data alone mostly can't prove a markup.
29. **Who pays for this?** Nobody, today, and the buyer shouldn't. A credit union might license a version with its name and posted rate, because the page already sends buyers to get a pre-approval. The partner demo shows that a sponsor can add a line but can't change a number. We haven't asked a credit union yet, so that's a hypothesis.
30. **Is the contract-box check legal advice?** No. It only checks that the contract's own numbers agree with each other, using the federal accuracy rule for the APR, and tells the buyer what to ask if they don't.
31. **You added seven lenders. Why do only 554,021 of 1,978,371 loans survive?** Two cleaning rules remove most of them (step 4). First, most loans from the six carmakers' finance arms carry a promotional rate the carmaker subsidises, which is not a market price, so 988,695 subsidised loans go. Second, bond pools also hold loans made more than 12 months before the filing, survivors of years of payoffs and defaults, so another 372,024 go (step 3). A business score or none removes 23,218 more, a missing or out-of-range score 39,024, and a few rows go for impossible values. The notebook prints every count. One deal, Nissan 2025-B, loses all 52,533 of its loans to the promotional-rate rule, so the loans come from 28 of the 29 deals. Toyota, Nissan and Capital One still add loans to the training and calibration months, but none of their loans fall in the test months; step 21 tests Toyota and Capital One on later deals instead.
